A celebration of the Riemann Hypothesis: the most beautiful open question in mathematics.

What is it?

In 1859, Bernhard Riemann studied the function ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + … and extended it to every complex number. He noticed that its zeros control how the prime numbers are spread out. He guessed that every non-trivial zero lies on a single vertical line, Re(s) = ½, the critical line.

Over 160 years later, no one has proved him right or wrong. That is the reason to celebrate it.

This is the Riemann–Siegel Z function along the critical line. Every time the curve crosses zero, it is a non-trivial zero of ζ.

Why primes care about zeros

Let π(x) count the primes up to x. Gauss guessed π(x) ≈ x/ln x, and a better guess is the logarithmic integral Li(x). Riemann showed the error between π(x) and Li(x) can be written as a sum over the zeros of ζ, so each zero adds a wave to the error. If the hypothesis is true, the error stays as small as it could be, roughly √x · ln x.

π(x) Li(x) x/ln x

Euler's product: where the connection starts

ζ(s) = Σ 1/nˢ = Πp prime 1/(1 − p−s)

Euler found that a sum over all integers equals a product over primes. This is the bridge between the zeta function and the primes.

Equivalent statements

The hypothesis can be rewritten in several ways that look unrelated.

Prime counting

|π(x) − Li(x)| < (1/8π)·√x·ln x for all x ≥ 2657 (Schoenfeld, 1976).

Mertens function

M(x) = Σ μ(n) grows no faster than x½+ε for every ε > 0.

Divisor sums

σ(n) ≤ Hn + eHn·ln Hn for all n ≥ 1 (Lagarias, 2002), where Hn is the n-th harmonic number.

Farey sequences

Fractions in lowest terms between 0 and 1 are distributed with error about N½+ε (Franel–Landau).

If it is true, if it is false

True

Primes are about as regularly distributed as possible. Hundreds of published theorems that assume it become unconditional.

False

A zero off the line would be a major discovery. Prime irregularities would be larger than we think, and we'd learn something deep about ζ.

Glossary

Complex number
A number a + bi where i² = −1.
Analytic continuation
Extending a function beyond where its formula converges. The series for ζ(s) only converges for Re(s) > 1, but ζ extends to the whole plane except s = 1.
Trivial zeros
ζ(s) = 0 at s = −2, −4, −6, … These are understood and not in question.
Non-trivial zeros
The other zeros, all known to lie in the critical strip 0 < Re(s) < 1. The hypothesis says they all have Re(s) = ½.
Critical line
The vertical line Re(s) = ½.
Functional equation
A symmetry relating ζ(s) and ζ(1 − s). It makes the zeros symmetric about the critical line.
Generalized RH
The same claim for a larger family of functions, the Dirichlet L-functions.

Reasons to celebrate

🔢 Primes

It's equivalent to a sharp bound on how far the prime counting function strays from its smooth estimate.

⚛️ Physics

The zeros' spacing matches the energy levels of heavy atomic nuclei (random matrix theory).

🖥️ Evidence

Over 1013 zeros have been checked (Gourdon, 2004), plus heights up to about 3×1012 (Platt & Trudgian, 2021). Every one sits on the line.

💰 A prize

It is one of the Clay Institute's Millennium Problems, worth $1,000,000.

    Timeline

    • 1859 Riemann publishes “On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude”.
    • 1896 Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin prove the Prime Number Theorem.
    • 1900 Hilbert lists it among his 23 problems.
    • 1914 Hardy proves infinitely many zeros lie on the line.
    • 1973 Montgomery and Dyson notice the zeros' spacing matches random matrix statistics.
    • 2000 The Clay Institute names it a Millennium Problem.

    Common misconceptions

    • “It's been proven.” No. Checking billions of zeros is strong evidence, not proof.
    • “It's only about zeros.” It is really a statement about how primes are distributed.
    • “ζ(−1) = −1/12 is the hypothesis.” That's a separate fact about analytic continuation.
    • “Someone claims a proof every year, so it's probably done.” Claimed proofs appear regularly, but none has been accepted by the mathematical community.
